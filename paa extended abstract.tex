\section{Introduction}

Neonatal mortality is a revealing measure of health inequality. Deaths in the first month of life reflect risks accumulated during pregnancy, conditions surrounding childbirth, and the quality of care during and after childbirth. Social disparities in neonatal mortality therefore are an important indicator for how inequalities in population health may stem from the very beginning of life.

In India, caste and religion are important dimensions of inequalities in health. This paper is the first, to our knowledge, that documents disparities in neonatal mortality across these dimensions in India, and finds that neonatal mortality rates among Dalit, Adivasi, and Other Backwards Class births are substantially higher than among forward caste births. 

Because the quality of healthcare during and after birth is an important determinant of neonatal survival, we compare these disparities across different healthcare settings. India has a large system of both rural public health facilities as well as private clinics and hospitals that offer obstetric care. Prior research has documented higher neonatal mortality among births occurring in private facilities than among those occurring in public facilities in Empowered Action Group states. This paper extends this work to show that disparities in neonatal mortality are substantially wider among private facility births than among public facility births in the Empowered Action Group states.

This pattern of wider disparities in neonatal mortality among private facility rather than public facility births may reflect differential selection into private care, including differences in the underlying risk of births or condition at arrival; sorting across private facilities of different quality; or differences in the care received by social groups within facilities. We begin to test these explanations by adjusting for observed risk factors and socioeconomic and geographic characteristics, and find that the larger disparities in neonatal mortality among private rather than public facility births persist after adjustments. 



and by examining differences in facility use, childbirth complications, newborn care practices, and delivery costs across social groups. 



One potential explanation for this pattern is differential selection into private facilities. Forward caste births are more likely than births to marginalized social groups to occur in private facilities, ruling out the possibility that the overall disparities simply reflect greater use of private care among marginalized groups. A remaining concern is that, among private-facility births, those to marginalized mothers may be systematically higher-risk than those to Forward caste mothers. We therefore estimate models that adjust for maternal and infant characteristics, socioeconomic status, geographic location, and survey round. The larger social-group disparities observed in private facilities persist after these adjustments, particularly in rural Uttar Pradesh and Bihar.
